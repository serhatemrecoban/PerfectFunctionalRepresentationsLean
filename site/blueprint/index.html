1. The representation problem

ISIT §I, equation (1) and its preceding decoder formulation.Formalized

# Definition · Section I, (1). Functional representation

A functional representation of (X, Y) is an auxiliary random variable Z such that X and Z are independent and there is a deterministic decoder f with Y = f(X, Z) on the support of the joint law.

For finite value alphabets, this is equivalent to I(X; Z) = 0 and H(Y | X, Z) = 0.

The structural definition permits unrestricted carriers. Its equivalence with the zero-information conditions requires finite-valued X, Y and Z, not a finite probability source. Decoding is required only on support.

# Identity and bound · Section I, (2)–(3). The information bound

For arbitrary finite-valued X, Y and Z,

I(Y; Z) = H(Y | X) − I(X; Z | Y) − H(Y | X, Z) + I(X; Z).

For a functional representation, the last two terms vanish, giving

I(Y; Z) = H(Y | X) − I(X; Z | Y) ≤ H(Y | X).

The four-term identity does not require a functional representation. Only its specialization and bound use that hypothesis. All three value alphabets are finite; the source space is unrestricted.

Proof idea

Expand mutual information by the chain rule in two orders. Independence and deterministic decoding remove the last two terms. Nonnegativity of conditional mutual information gives the upper bound.

# Definition and equality criterion · Section I. Perfectness is the Markov equality case

A functional representation Z is perfect when I(Y; Z) = H(Y | X). Among functional representations, the following are equivalent:

I(Y; Z) = H(Y | X) ⇔ I(X; Z | Y) = 0 ⇔ X → Y → Z.

Finite-valued X, Y and Z are assumed. The independence and decoding requirements remain part of perfectness; an information equality or Markov condition alone is not a functional representation. Null output fibres impose no extra conditional-law claim.

Proof idea

The information identity turns equality in the bound into zero conditional mutual information. LeanInfoTheory's finite-information Markov characterization identifies this with conditional independence of X and Z given Y.

2. Canonical representations

ISIT §II-A, equation (5), Definition 1 and its intended evaluation decoder.Formalized

# Definition 1. Canonical representations and couplings

A canonical representation is a random function W from inputs to outputs, independent of X, that recovers Y by evaluation: Y = W(X) on support. A law q on such functions is a canonical coupling of a channel K when the coordinate W(x) has law K(x) for every input x.

These are distinct contracts. Correct coordinate marginals alone do not ensure independence or evaluation decoding for an already fixed triple. Conversely, a canonical representation of a fixed pair determines the channel rows only on that pair's input support.

# Lemma 1. Canonicalization preserves information

Every functional representation Z with finite-valued X, Y and Z has a canonical replacement W on the same source, with the same X and Y, such that

I(Y; W) = I(Y; Z).

The original auxiliary Z is finite-valued in the information theorem. The unrestricted-auxiliary reading of the manuscript is not covered. The probability source itself need not be finite.

Proof idea

Choose a support-correct decoder f and set W(x) = f(x, Z) for every input x. Independence and evaluation decoding follow from the original representation. Apply the information identity to Z and W. Since W is a function of Z, ordinary and conditional data processing force the resulting two differences to have opposite signs, hence both are zero.

3. Perfect channels and singularity

ISIT §II-A, Definition 2 and its continuation explaining the common posterior and Markov property.Formalized

# Definition 2. Perfect representability of a channel

A channel K is perfectly representable when there are one law q of a random decoder W and one posterior channel V from outputs to decoder vectors such that, for every input x, the joint law of (W(x), W) under q equals the law obtained by sampling y from K(x) and then W from V(y).

This single posterior must work for every input row.

This is a prior-free structural definition with nonempty input. It does not assume finite alphabets or a chosen input law. Lemma 1 helps relate finite pair perfectness to this definition; it is not a prerequisite for defining the all-row factorization.

# Remark 1 and supporting bridge. Channel perfectness and the input prior

For a finite perfectly representable channel, one canonical coupling law produces a perfect canonical joint for every input prior. Conversely, for a specified full-support input prior p, the pair law p(x)K(y | x) is perfectly representable if and only if K is perfectly representable.

Input and output alphabets are finite and input is nonempty. Reusing a coupling means forming fresh canonical joints, not asserting independence of an existing random variable from arbitrary new variables. Pair representability permits a finite auxiliary on an extension of the pair law. Without full input support, the converse concerns the channel restricted to that support.

Proof idea

The common posterior factors the canonical joint for any prior, so the Markov equality criterion gives perfectness. For the converse, canonicalize a finite perfect pair witness, recover every row using full input support, and extract its common posterior.

# Definition 3. Singular channels

A channel is singular if any two positive entries in a fixed output column are equal:

K(y | x₁) > 0 and K(y | x₂) > 0 ⇒ K(y | x₁) = K(y | x₂).

Singularity here is a columnwise probability condition, not a determinant condition. The predicate needs neither finite alphabets nor an input prior; an entirely zero column imposes no condition.

# Lemma 2(a). Perfect channels must be singular

Every perfectly representable channel is singular.

This prior-free proof requires no finite-alphabet assumption. Nonempty input is inherited from the channel-perfectness definition. No cancellation of a zero posterior mass is used.

Proof idea

Fix an output emitted by two rows and choose a vector in its common posterior support. The factorization forces that vector to decode to the chosen output at both inputs. Comparing the same positive vector mass in the two factorizations shows that the two channel entries agree.

# Lemma 2(b). Singularity suffices for two inputs

For a finite channel with exactly two inputs, singularity is sufficient for perfect representability. Together with part (a), perfect representability and singularity are equivalent.

The output alphabet is finite and the input has cardinality exactly two. Globally unused outputs receive a total posterior without imposing a false positive-mass condition. Only the explicit coupling construction, not its mismatch optimality, is used for sufficiency.

Proof idea

Use the explicit maximal coupling of the two rows. At a shared output, singularity makes the supported decoder vector constant. At an output exclusive to one row, the input is determined. These cases yield one posterior for both rows, hence a perfect channel.

# Lemma 2(c). Singularity suffices for two active outputs

For a finite channel with nonempty input and at most two active outputs, singularity is sufficient for perfect representability. Together with part (a), the two properties are equivalent.

Both alphabets are finite. The formal result counts the union of row supports, so unused labels do not matter and singleton active output is included. This is a separate proof from the binary-input coupling argument.

Proof idea

After restricting to active outputs, singularity forces either identical rows or a deterministic channel. In the first case, a random constant decoder is perfect; in the second, the deterministic decoder vector is perfect. Transport the witness back to the ambient output alphabet.

# Example 1. Singularity is not sufficient in general

The channel with rows (½, ½, 0), (½, 0, ½), and (0, ½, ½) is singular but is not perfectly representable.

The negative statement rules out every possible canonical law and common posterior, not merely one candidate coupling. This is a direct support argument; it does not use Theorem 1 or an LP certificate.

Proof idea

Assume a common-posterior witness exists. Choose a supported decoder vector whose first coordinate selects the first output. The shared posterior forces its second coordinate to select that output too. Its third coordinate must select one of the remaining two outputs; either choice forces an incompatible value in an earlier coordinate. Thus no witness exists.

# Supporting lemma · Total variation and coupling. Total variation is the minimum mismatch

For finite laws p and q, define TV(p, q) = ½∑y|p(y) − q(y)|. Every coupling (U, V) of these laws satisfies

Pr[U ≠ V] ≥ TV(p, q),

and an explicitly constructed maximal coupling attains equality.

The alphabet is finite and both marginals are verified exactly. Equal laws, disjoint supports and zero-probability symbols are included. This supplies a witness, not just an infimum formula.

Proof idea

A coupling's diagonal mass at y is at most min(p(y), q(y)); summing bounds its agreement probability. Put that common mass on the diagonal and couple the residual masses after normalization. Their disjoint supports make the residual contribution entirely off-diagonal. Handle zero residual mass separately.

# Supporting lemma · Binary canonical entropy. Decoder mismatch determines conditional entropy

For a binary input X and a canonical decoder vector W = (W₁, W₂),

H(Y | W) = Pr[W₁ ≠ W₂] H(X).

Consequently I(Y; W) = H(Y) − Pr[W₁ ≠ W₂] H(X).

The output alphabet is finite. The identity allows every binary input prior, including point masses, and needs no additional full-support or prescribed-channel-marginal assumption. It does not assign a particular entropy to arbitrary completions of null fibres.

Proof idea

Condition on a positive-mass decoder vector. If its coordinates agree, the output is constant. Otherwise evaluation is an injective relabeling of the binary input, whose law is unchanged by independence. Sum these fibre entropies with their probabilities; zero-mass fibres contribute zero.

# Lemma 3(a). The attained binary information optimum

Let X have a binary input alphabet and let r be the total variation distance between the two specified channel rows. Every finite-auxiliary functional representation satisfies

I(Y; Z) ≤ H(Y) − rH(X).

The canonical representation built from the maximal coupling attains this bound, so it is an actual maximum within the formalized finite-auxiliary class.

The input is binary, the output and original auxiliary are finite-valued, and the source space is unrestricted. The bound and attainment allow every input prior. The attaining joint may live on a new source; no optimizer on an arbitrary pre-existing source is promised. Infinite auxiliaries are not covered.

Proof idea

For a full-support input prior, canonicalize any candidate and apply the mismatch-entropy identity and the universal coupling bound. A point prior has H(X) = 0 and is handled without recovering an unseen channel row. For attainment, use the specified maximal coupling as the decoder-vector law and verify its representation property, exact pair law and information value.

# Lemma 3(b). When the binary optimum is perfect

For a finite binary-input channel and a full-support input prior,

rH(X) = I(X; Y) ⇔ the channel is singular.

Thus the attained binary optimum reaches H(Y | X) exactly for singular channels. Singularity implies the equality for every prior, even without full support.

Full input support is essential for the ambient-channel converse: a degenerate prior cannot detect an unused row. The statement concerns finite output alphabets, with r defined from the actual two channel rows.

Proof idea

The universal representation bound applied to the attaining witness gives I(X; Y) ≤ rH(X). A singular binary channel has a perfect canonical witness, giving the reverse inequality. Conversely, numerical equality makes the attaining pair perfect; the full-support pair/channel bridge and necessary singularity recover singularity of the whole channel.

# Four operation definitions. Legal splits and merges

  • PPS: split a column in a ratio 0 < t < 1, retaining its pattern.
  • PPM: merge two columns with the same pattern, adding their values.
  • VPS: split a column's support into two nonempty parts, retaining its value.
  • VPM: merge two equal-value columns with disjoint supports.

Operations act on active finite singular channels with finite nonempty input and retain stochastic normalization, singularity and specified column order. VPS admits value one; this follows the intended construction rather than the paper's unnecessarily strict printed value range.

# Supporting lemma. Recovering an operation's source

PPS and PPM recover one another when the split ratio is retained; VPS and VPM recover one another when the support mask is retained. The recovered endpoint agrees with the original through a specified output bijection. These recoveries extend to witnessed finite paths.

The legal channels have finite nonempty input and active finite output. Merging forgets information: this is recovery with witnesses, not a globally inverse function on bare channels or an assertion that arbitrary masks are unique.

Proof idea

Calculate the affected and unaffected column entries, prove the recovered split is legal, and keep the directed output comparison. Reverse a path by reversing its edges and composing those comparisons.

# Theorem 1. Constructive characterization

An active finite channel is perfectly representable exactly when it can be built from the one-output all-ones channel by PPS/VPS/PPM operations. Equivalently, it can be reduced to that seed by PPS/VPM/PPM operations.

# Supporting lemma. Value one forces equality

Every primal-feasible objective is at most one. A feasible vector attains one exactly when all incidence constraints are tight: Hw = v.

Input is finite and nonempty; nonempty occurring patterns and nonnegative slack are essential. The result concerns the same coefficient vector, including its zero coordinates.

Proof idea

Normalization gives P(v − Hw) = (1 − ∑j wj)1. Nonnegative slack implies the upper bound. If the objective is one, every nonempty pattern contains an input whose zero slack sum forces that pattern's slack coordinate to vanish.

# Supporting lemmas. Weak duality and a value-one witness

Every primal-feasible objective is bounded above by every dual-feasible objective. The indicator of patterns containing any fixed input is dual-feasible and has objective one.

Input is finite and nonempty, so an input-row witness is available. A dual-feasible vector of value one exists for every such valid compressed datum, even nonperfect data. Its existence alone is therefore not the perfectness criterion; it must also be a least value.

Proof idea

Multiply the incidence inequalities by nonnegative coordinates and rearrange finite sums to obtain weak duality. An exact partition contains the fixed input in exactly one block; row normalization gives the witness's objective one.
